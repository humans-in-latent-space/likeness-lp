# Introduction

We are living through a structural transformation driven by the accelerating scale and cultural reach of artificial intelligence—a shift fraught with tangible anxieties: job displacement, resource depletion, institutional opacity, and the proliferation of deepfakes.

Among the starker symptoms of this shift is the deluge of "AI slop" flooding digital streaming platforms (DSPs) like Spotify and Apple Music: mass-produced synthetic tracks generated with minimal effort to harvest micro-royalties. The underlying phenomenon is not entirely unprecedented—it has existed in various guises since the rise of commercial library music and stock asset production—yet generative models have magnified it to an industrial scale. Compounded by the opacity of streaming platforms, listeners reasonably conclude that AI in music is primarily an instrument of deception, and that every AI-generated song is inevitably soulless, instant kitsch. A cursory search on YouTube yields countless tutorials demonstrating how to become an "AI artist" overnight—whether pitched as an exposé on a broken system or hawked as a passive-income cheat code.

What remains largely absent are serious, constructive attempts to treat generative AI as a legitimate medium for creative expression. What happens when we reject volume and speed as metrics, replacing them with aesthetic intentionality and artistic accountability?

**Theory speculates; the workshop reveals.**

This documentation records a personal expedition into latent space. It offers a process-oriented account of the conception and synthetic realization of *Likeness*, a concept album comprising thirteen [tracks](./70_00_tracks.md). I do not approach this as a theoretical metastudy. Rather, this project operates firmly within the tradition of *practice-as-research* and *critical making*: insight achieved through workshop labor. Some systems can only be understood by building them; theoretical abstractions that remain detached from direct material practice too often fail to substantiate their claims.

The following pages document my methodology, my technical workflow, and the human intent embedded within a thoroughly synthetic artifact. I disclose the final prompts and operational parameters for every track on the album. At the same time, this is not a turnkey pipeline, a collection of scripts, or a set of copy-paste templates. There is no magical machine in this repository designed to spit out art at the press of a button—an ambition that represents an inherent contradiction in terms. Consider this an itinerary. Anyone is free to adapt this methodology, but doing so demands time, labor, and critical reflection. The objective here is not effortless reproducibility, but radical transparency. I think of this as *Declared Intent*: The point is neither to mystify nor over-inflate my artistic vision, but to demarcate clearly what was shaped by the human and what was generated by the machine.

Over the course of this five-month endeavor (three for the album creation, another two for this documentation), I encountered structural, practical, and ethical friction. How does an artist give back when drawing—even indirectly—from models trained on the collective labor of countless human creators? How do you demonstrate authorship and craft when the generative surface is algorithmic? And how do you maintain transparency when streaming distributors prefer to bury disclosures in the fine print?

**Transparency is not a regulatory obligation—it is a personal ethos.**

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, whose second phase took effect on August 2, 2026, represents a crucial milestone in countering AI-driven deception. Article 50 establishes extensive transparency mandates. Yet [Article 50 (4)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/#p-article-50-4) strictly targets *deepfakes*—the deliberate impersonation of a specific, real-world entity—rendering the statute inapplicable to generic AI slop. While [Article 50 (2)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/#p-article-50-2) mandates machine-readable provenance (such as C2PA metadata or SynthID watermarking), this is virtually impossible for listeners to verify in practice—whether because streaming platforms fail to parse the tags, or because lossy compression strips the headers and watermarks entirely.

The consequences are severe: rather than curbing AI slop, the EU AI Act leaves a regulatory vacuum in its wake. On major DSPs, traditional songwriting, meticulously curated synthetic productions, and zero-effort commodity tracks sit side by side, ostensibly indistinguishable. The remedy cannot simply be the imposition of blunt warning labels akin to "May contain traces of AI." Such badges merely stigmatize generative tools indiscriminately while offering zero insight to the listener. AI is already pervasive; within a few years, a generic disclaimer will lose all explanatory power.

In my view, creators who treat generative models as instruments for realizing an authentic artistic vision must distance themselves from AI slop through radical, proactive transparency. They must voluntarily exceed statutory minimums, laying bare their conception and creative methodology. Slop purveyors simply cannot do this: their entire business model depends on operating anonymously, maximizing throughput with negligible effort. Conversely, investing genuine labor into documenting the conception behind a work demonstrates that the creator is not hiding their reliance on synthetic tools, but embracing them as an integral facet of their craft.

Ultimately, this entire repository represents my personal vision of what meaningful disclosure can look like.

**The human condition cannot be surveyed from a desk—it has to be inhabited.**

This documentation also demands an honest account of the project's genesis. In May 2026, the birth of my second child was imminent. I belong to that category of expectant fathers who must stay actively occupied to avoid succumbing to anxiety for their partner or the paralysis of waiting for the delivery date. For me, distraction requires active creation rather than passive consumption—reading or streaming simply will not do. Programming would normally be my natural outlet, but code demands a desk, a keyboard, and dedicated focus. With a toddler to care for and a household to support, I needed a creative medium that could exist in the margins: literally between doorframes, in the fragmentary five-minute pockets that family life affords.

It had to live on a mobile phone. That constraint alone led me to the Gemini mobile application, which is architected for asynchronous, on-the-go interaction. After our daughter was born, I spent long night shifts pacing dark hallways with the baby asleep in a sling, typing. Later, I mastered the final tracks at a standing desk with her still strapped to my chest. I recount this not for sentimental effect, but because it is fundamental to the work: *Likeness* was not conceived as a sanitized terminal exercise or a desktop experiment. It was an intensely personal engagement with a specific chapter of my life. The album is not a traditional, academic case study; it has a narrative pulse, and I care deeply about every track. I would never have published it had it failed to meet my own aesthetic standards. Five months later, I still listen to it regularly. Listeners are entirely free to dislike it—musical taste remains fiercely personal—but it should not be dismissed as something less than art simply because of the tools used to make it.
